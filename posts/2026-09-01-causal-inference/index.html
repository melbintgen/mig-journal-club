Causal inference starts with the question

Three papers argue that causal inference begins by specifying the intervention, comparator, population and target trial. What remains possible when biological studies cannot meet every causal assumption?

Dickerman BA, García-Albéniz X, Logan RW, Denaxas S, Hernán MA. Avoidable flaws in observational analyses: an application to statins and cancer. Nature Medicine 25(10):1601–1606 (2019). doi:10.1038/s41591-019-0597-x

Presented by Jiadong Mao · 1 September 2026 · Slides

The question comes before the method

Together, the three papers make a case for treating causal inference first as a discipline of question-setting. A causal question must say what intervention is being compared with what alternative, for whom, over what period, and against which outcome. Choosing a model before settling those points does not merely leave details unspecified; it can change the question being answered.

Dickerman and colleagues provide the sharpest demonstration. Using electronic health records from 733,804 UK adults, they compared statin initiation with no initiation. An analysis that classified people by whether they accumulated more than four years of statin use produced a hazard ratio of 0.22 for total cancer, suggesting a very large protective association. The classification used future information: to enter the long-term-user group, a person had to remain alive, cancer-free and under follow-up long enough to accumulate that exposure. This created immortal time bias.

When eligibility, treatment assignment and the start of follow-up were aligned at time zero to emulate a target trial, the corresponding intention-to-treat hazard ratio was 1.02. The same flaw reversed the apparent direction of the association with type 2 diabetes: defining exposure by observed duration gave a hazard ratio of 0.20, whereas the target-trial estimate was 1.11. The lesson is not that observational data cannot support causal inference. It is that a large dataset and a sophisticated analysis cannot repair a comparison defined using information that would have been unavailable when treatment was assigned.

Four targets expose what the estimate means

Lu and colleagues proposed four targets for causal inference on observation data. The target estimand states the treatment effect of interest, including the intervention, comparator, outcome and follow-up. The target population states to whom the answer is intended to apply. The target trial is the hypothetical pragmatic randomised trial the observational analysis seeks to emulate. Target validity then asks whether the estimate is valid for that estimand and population, combining the usual concerns about internal and external validity.

This framework shifts difficult conversations to the beginning of a study. “Do statins prevent cancer?” is not yet a complete causal question: initiation and sustained treatment are different interventions, dose and adherence require definitions, and the relevant population and follow-up period must be fixed. Changing these choices after seeing the result changes the estimand and invites cherry-picking.

The discussion connected these targets to three identification assumptions. Consistency requires the intervention and outcome to be defined well enough that an observed treatment corresponds to the treatment being analysed. Positivity requires both treatment strategies to be possible for every relevant kind of participant. Exchangeability requires the comparison groups not to differ, after conditioning on measured information, in other causes of the outcome. Health-seeking behaviour illustrates the last problem: people who take preventive medication may also seek care, undergo screening or change behaviour in ways that affect their cancer risk.

Making assumptions explicit is useful, but does not make them true. An analysis can therefore supply a causal answer only conditional on assumptions that data alone may not verify. Disagreement about a causal conclusion may persist because attendees accept different assumptions, rather than because one side has chosen the wrong estimator.

Cell experiments intervene, but design still sets the claim

The same reasoning applies to single-cell experiments without requiring every study to adopt the machinery of observational epidemiology. A pathway-inhibition experiment can specify an intervention, a matched control, an outcome at a fixed time and a target population of donors. In that sense, experimental biologists already reason causally whenever they perturb a system and compare outcomes.

The causal framing nevertheless reveals familiar failure modes. Cells are not independent biological replicates when treatment is assigned at the donor or culture level. Quality-control filtering can also become post-treatment selection: if an intervention changes RNA abundance or cell viability, applying the same threshold may selectively remove the cells most affected by treatment. Finally, a result across a small set of donors does not automatically transport to the population the biological claim names.

One unresolved point was whether formal causal inference is necessary for useful biological or clinical work. Yeganeh Khazaei argued that action can be justified by a sufficiently strong body of evidence without every study establishing a causal effect in isolation. Others stressed that discovery experiments often leave the question flexible and use a later perturbation as validation, which does not necessarily reproduce a pre-specified target trial. Jiadong’s proposed middle ground was causal inference as an attitude to design: ask what would have to be compared, make the assumptions visible, and state how far the resulting claim can travel.

Evidence does not come from one culture

Swales’ three “cultures” supplied a broader account of evidence: mechanistic biomedicine asks whether an intervention could work, clinical experience asks how it behaves in whole patients, and numerical evidence estimates whether it does work across groups. Each can correct the others. Homeopathy has had apparently favourable numerical results without a credible mechanism or clinical acceptance. Conversely, suppressing ventricular arrhythmias after myocardial infarction had mechanistic and clinical plausibility, yet randomised evidence showed that particular antiarrhythmic drugs increased mortality.

The biological analogue of clinical evidence remained unsettled. For cell and animal studies it might include accumulated domain knowledge about the model, the experimental design and the conditions under which results have previously translated. It would not replace direct evidence in patients, but it still constrains which causal accounts are plausible. No resolution was reached on how the three cultures should be weighted, or on what their precise analogues are outside clinical medicine.

Meta-analysis does not remove this design problem. Pooling studies that define exposure, time zero or eligible populations incompatibly can make a biased answer more precise. Yeganeh noted that when clinical studies are highly heterogeneous, a systematic or scoping review may be more defensible than forcing a pooled estimate. Inclusion criteria and the target question must therefore be settled before synthesis, just as they must be settled before modelling a single cohort.

Follow-up reading

Yeganeh proposed adaptive platform trials as a practical extension to the discussion. These trials use a master protocol to evaluate several interventions and can add or drop arms under pre-specified decision rules. A general entry point is Angus DC, Alexander BM, Berry S, et al. Adaptive platform trials: definition, design, conduct and reporting considerations. Nature Reviews Drug Discovery 18:797–807 (2019). doi:10.1038/s41573-019-0034-3
